The Double Helix

DNA (Deoxyribonucleic Acid) is the hereditary material in humans and almost all other organisms. Its iconic double helix structure was first described by Watson and Crick in 1953.

  • Two complementary strands wound around each other
  • Right-handed helical structure
  • Approximately 3.4 Ångströms between base pairs

Sugar-Phosphate Backbone

The structural framework of DNA consists of alternating sugar (deoxyribose) and phosphate groups. This backbone provides stability and protection for the genetic information stored in the bases.

  • Phosphate groups create negative charge
  • Deoxyribose sugar connects to bases
  • Forms the outer "rails" of the DNA ladder

Nitrogenous Bases

The four bases - Adenine (A), Thymine (T), Guanine (G), and Cytosine (C) - store the genetic information. They form the "rungs" of the DNA ladder through hydrogen bonding.

A ≡ T 2 bonds
G ≡ C 3 bonds

Hydrogen Bonds

Weak hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs hold the two strands together. These bonds are strong enough to maintain structure but weak enough to allow separation during replication.

  • A-T pairs form 2 hydrogen bonds
  • G-C pairs form 3 hydrogen bonds
  • Enable DNA replication and transcription

Base Pairing Rules

Chargaff's rules describe the complementary base pairing that ensures genetic fidelity. This complementarity is crucial for DNA replication and repair mechanisms.

  • Adenine always pairs with Thymine
  • Guanine always pairs with Cytosine
  • Purine always pairs with Pyrimidine
  • Equal amounts of A=T and G=C in double-stranded DNA
